Euro standards: clean exhaust, empty soul

A motorcycle can be faster, cleaner and better equipped than ever, yet give you less pleasure. What remains when the sound fades, the response is smoothed out and a Netflix-sized screen grows above the handlebars?

Okretaji · Opinion · 5 November 2026

We also ride motorcycles for things a catalogue cannot capture. The way the engine pulls out of a corner. The sound of the intake as you open the throttle. The rhythm you feel through the seat and handlebars.

That is why a less powerful motorcycle can leave more memories than one that beats it in every category on paper. And it keeps inviting you to ride the same road again.

Sound is part of the ride

Discussions about sound turn too quickly to straight pipes and neighbours who cannot sleep. Yet there is plenty of room between a characterless hum and making an entire street miserable.

Sound has a tone, a rhythm and a way of building with the revs. The pulse of a twin, the changing note of a four-cylinder engine, the intake under load: all contribute to the experience. You can enjoy listening to an interesting engine at perfectly normal speeds.

When that voice disappears, the motorcycle becomes anonymous, and the rider loses part of the pleasure that made them get on it in the first place. Efficiency simply cannot replace feeling.

Precision matters here: Euro 5 limits harmful exhaust emissions, while noise is also governed by separate type-approval requirements. The final sound also depends on intake and exhaust design. The Euro label therefore cannot explain every change we dislike.

Nevertheless, the buyer sees the final result. It is perfectly reasonable to expect a factory-standard, type-approved motorcycle to have a sound you want to hear again.

Character starts beneath your right hand

Character is also the way an engine responds. How it pulls from low revs, where it comes alive and how much it encourages you to reach for the next gear. Those differences make people choose particular engines and remain loyal to them for years.

Electronic throttle control gives manufacturers considerable freedom to shape that relationship. It can deliver a precise, natural response. It can also make you feel as if someone between your hand and the rear wheel is constantly adjusting your throttle cable.

Nostalgia can easily flatter the past. A snatchy throttle, numb fingers and a morning struggle to get started are not qualities worth preserving. What deserves to survive is whatever puts a spark in your eye when you ride, while removing what makes riding unnecessarily difficult.

How much can we blame on Euro 5?

Cleaner exhaust is a real gain. But stricter requirements also mean more development, testing and design changes. Their effect on the choice of engines is particularly interesting: is it worth developing something distinctive for fewer buyers, or is it easier to offer another version of an existing, developed design?

That question deserves discussion. We do not need old statistics about motorcycles accounting for a small share of CO₂ to have it. Climate emissions and substances that harm health in cities are different issues.

Manufacturers share the responsibility. Regulations set the conditions; development teams and management decide what to build within them. When a motorcycle loses what people loved about it, the word ecology is too little of an explanation.

I want an engine's recognisable character to be treated as a development goal. Just as seriously as power, fuel consumption and equipment.

While we discuss what we can no longer hear, something above the handlebars is growing harder and harder to ignore.

A screen.

Soon touring-bike launches will explain how well the display handles dark scenes and whether Netflix comes as standard. The handlebars get more buttons, the menus gain more layers, and the rider gets homework before the first trip.

A good display with legible instruments and clear navigation can be an excellent companion. The trouble begins when an ordinary action becomes a tour of the menus. When you have to search for information instead of seeing it. When animations and notifications obscure a basic function.

It may all look impressive in the showroom. In the rain, wearing gloves and needing to get your eyes back on the road, the criteria are simpler: can I immediately see what I need, and can I do what I want?

Screen size is easy to put in a catalogue. Natural throttle response is much harder. I worry that what is easier to demonstrate at a presentation too often takes priority over what you discover after two hundred kilometres.

And Euro 5 never ordered anyone to fit a television. The industry signs that part of the bill itself.

Even an old Vespa deserves a sensible discussion

Milan shows how attitudes towards motorcycles are more complicated than a label on a registration document. The city postponed the start of the ban in Areas B and C for Euro 2 and 3 two-strokes and Euro 0, 1 and 2 four-strokes until 1 October 2027.

Residents' health is a legitimate reason for restrictions. At the same time, a historic Vespa used for occasional rides deserves a discussion that considers how much it is actually used. Its cultural value does not cancel its emissions, but it can justify carefully considered exemptions.

That argument carries more weight than claiming an old vehicle does not pollute because we love it. Precisely because we love it, we should be able to defend it with better arguments.

A reason to look back once more

I expect a modern motorcycle to have good brakes, reliable starting, quality suspension and electronics that help when needed. Alongside all that, I want a reason for a Sunday ride with no destination.

A sound I recognise. An engine with its own rhythm. A corner exit that stays with me longer than the welcome animation.

Manufacturers know how to show that a motorcycle is cleaner, faster and more connected. I would like them to show just as convincingly why I will want to ride it as soon as I find a little time.

When I get off, I want to turn back, look at the motorcycle and smile. As for the screen in my living room, I already have a remote.
