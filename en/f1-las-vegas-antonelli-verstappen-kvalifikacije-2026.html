Antonelli fastest in Las Vegas, Verstappen on pole: how a penalty changed qualifying

Antonelli 1:33.893, Verstappen +0.258 seconds. But the front row does not look like the qualifying results.

Okretaji · 21 November 2026 · Editorial

The fastest lap in qualifying is not always enough for pole position. Kimi Antonelli experienced just that in Las Vegas: he topped the timesheets, but will start the race from the third row. Max Verstappen, despite being slower by more than a quarter of a second, starts from pole position. Behind this unusual outcome lie two things that today are worth almost as much as pure car speed: the limited number of power unit components and the help of a slipstream on long straights.

Antonelli set a 1:33.893 in the final qualifying session. Verstappen's best lap was 1:34.151, Russell's 1:34.224, and Norris's 1:34.244. Just 0.351 seconds separated the top four drivers. The order on the stopwatch was clear; the starting order will be different.

The reason is Antonelli's five-place grid penalty for power unit changes. From first place, he drops to sixth. Verstappen moves up to first, Russell to second, Norris to third, Hadjar to fourth, and rookie Arvid Lindblad to fifth. Oscar Piastri, seventh in qualifying, also carries a ten-place penalty for a power unit change and is set to start seventeenth.

Why did Mercedes have the fastest lap, but not the best starting position?

Formula 1 does not reward just one perfect lap. Teams have a limited number of individual power unit components available throughout the season. When they exceed the allowed quota, a driver can receive a grid drop penalty. Such a penalty does not erase the qualifying lap time: Antonelli remains the fastest in the qualifying results, but not first on the starting grid.

This is why Mercedes' qualifying result is not a simple comparison of who has the strongest engine. An earlier plan with an upgraded turbocharger, the differing usage of permitted components between Antonelli and Russell, and penalties for power unit changes show how much technical decisions affect the result. It is important, however, to distinguish between the turbo upgrade and the penalty for a specific component: one must not claim without FIA documentation that the new turbocharger design itself caused Antonelli's penalty.

Same car, different lap: the slipstream also decides in Las Vegas

The long straights of Las Vegas make the slipstream extremely valuable. A driver following closely behind the car ahead encounters air that has already been partially displaced by that car. Aerodynamic drag can be reduced, and straight-line speed can increase. But there is another side: following too closely in corners can disrupt aerodynamic grip and tyre temperatures.

This compromise was visible in the closing stages of qualifying. According to the official Formula 1 report, Verstappen benefited from a slipstream on his first Q3 flying lap, while Antonelli went out for his final run almost at the very last moment. The Italian himself pointed out after qualifying how much help the slipstream was. Therefore, the 0.258-second gap does not represent a pure, isolated comparison of Mercedes and Red Bull engine performance.

Ferrari's problem further changes the race

Charles Leclerc and Lewis Hamilton got stuck in Q2, qualifying 11th and 12th. That is not just a statistically poor result. On a circuit with long straights and heavy braking zones, they will have to fight through traffic, and their strategy will depend on how quickly they can make up positions and preserve their tyres.

Who is the real winner of Saturday's outcome?

On the stopwatch — Antonelli. In the battle for grid position — Verstappen. And Mercedes? Its car showed front-row pace, but at the same time must deal with the consequences of the seasonal power unit arithmetic.

This is also why Verstappen should not be declared the favourite in advance based on pole position alone. Antonelli proved he can set the fastest lap, and Russell starts alongside Max. Sunday's story is therefore not just the sixth-placed driver hunting the leader: it is a contest between pure pace, track position, tyres, and the decisions teams made weeks before Las Vegas.
