Why do some automatics shift gears while others sound like a scooter?

You press the throttle, revs rise and stay high, and the car keeps accelerating. A CVT works differently from a traditional automatic — and manufacturers have a reason for that.

For the driver, the process is the same: move the selector to D, press the accelerator and go. Yet behind the "automatic" label lie different designs. A traditional automatic features preset gear ratios, usually combined with a torque converter. A DSG uses two clutches and also a specific number of gears. A CVT changes ratios continuously, without necessarily shifting from first to second and then third.

The engine doesn't have to follow the gearbox's rhythm

With fixed-ratio transmissions, engine revs rise with the car's speed and drop after shifting up. A CVT has more freedom: it adjusts the ratio so the engine operates in the range that matches the driver's demand. During gentle driving, it can maintain lower revs, while during hard acceleration, it keeps the engine in the required power band.

This opens up room for smoother driving and lower fuel consumption. However, better engine efficiency doesn't automatically mean the transmission itself is more efficient. In its technical description of the Xtronic system, Nissan distinguishes precisely between these two things: engine efficiency and transmission losses.

How does a traditional automatic work?

In a conventional traditional automatic, different gears are created using planetary gear sets. Clutches and brakes connect or hold their individual parts to change the gear ratio. The torque converter enables power transmission via fluid and a smooth take-off; a lock-up clutch reduces slip under appropriate conditions.

How does a CVT change ratios without shifting?

In a typical automotive CVT, two variable-diameter pulleys are connected by a metal belt or chain. By moving the conical halves closer together or further apart, the position of the belt or chain changes, and with it the gear ratio. The pulleys don't grow: the diameter at which the belt operates on them changes.

When pulling away, the belt operates on a smaller diameter of the driving pulley and a larger diameter of the driven pulley. For a longer ratio, this relationship changes. Between the extreme positions, the ratio adjusts continuously. Nissan uses steel-belt versions, while Subaru Lineartronic uses a chain.

Why does it sound like it's struggling?

Drivers are accustomed to revs rising, a gear change, and then the engine noise dropping. A CVT can break this rhythm: it raises the revs and holds them there as the car continues to accelerate. Such behaviour in itself is not proof of a fault or slipping. The transmission can maintain the engine in the required power band precisely in this way.

Some designs therefore simulate gear steps. The electronics intentionally create steps to make the sound and acceleration feel more familiar.

Why doesn't everyone use it?

The belt or chain must transmit the load between the pulleys, and pressure management and transmission operation create losses. The design must be adapted to torque, load and cooling. The choice of gearbox therefore depends on the car as a whole and the character the manufacturer wants to achieve.

Nor is a CVT a single unchanging formula. With the Direct Shift-CVT, Toyota added a separate gear drive for pull-away. This improves efficiency at low speeds, after which the continuously variable part takes over.

Toyota's hybrid e-CVT doesn't have that belt

Toyota's hybrid e-CVT with a power split device doesn't change ratios by moving a belt across pulleys. It uses a planetary gear set and electric machines that jointly manage power delivery. To the driver, it may behave similarly to a CVT, but its construction is different. Therefore, experiences with a particular belt-driven CVT should not be automatically applied to this hybrid transmission.

Manufacturers choose a CVT for its smoothness and greater freedom in controlling engine operation. A driver who likes to feel every gear change might prefer a different automatic. The absence of shifting is the fundamental idea of a CVT.
