Stoner and Márquez: same instinct, different MotoGP era

Why are two champions often mentioned together when talking about riding on the absolute limit? One clue leads to left-hand corners, the other to their ability to extract speed from a motorcycle that was not always easy to ride.

Okretaji · Original editorial explanation · 29 October 2026.

Casey Stoner and Marc Márquez did not race in the same technical era, so a comparison cannot be a simple head-to-head snapshot. Yet, they are linked by a few concrete things: dirt-track riding experience, the ability to handle slides and exceptionally strong statistics on anti-clockwise circuits.

Why are left-hand corners interesting?

In an analysis published in Motor Sport Magazine in July 2026, drawing on data from analyst Dimitrios Stathopoulos, Márquez's win percentage on anti-clockwise circuits was cited at approximately 61%, and Stoner's at 44%. On clockwise circuits, their figures were closer to other top riders: around 25–27%.

One possible explanation is their dirt-track experience, where a rider learns to control a motorcycle while tyres slide on low-grip surfaces. Such training develops a feel for weight transfer, motorcycle positioning and the moment to let the rear wheel step out. This does not mean dirt-track is the sole reason for their results — it is one piece of a broader picture.

Márquez studied Stoner's data

In an interview reported by Crash.net in 2022, Márquez said that at the beginning of his MotoGP career he studied Stoner's data and tried to adopt parts of his approach. He specifically mentioned Turn 3 at Phillip Island, the fast left-hander where Stoner was exceptionally successful. This is a direct link between the two — not just a fan comparison.

But copying a line or a movement does not mean riding identically. Márquez emphasised in the same interview that motorcycles have changed, especially due to aerodynamics and downforce. A technique that suited one generation of motorcycle does not necessarily translate one-to-one to a newer one.

What can you see in the footage?

  • Corner entry: compare how late the rider brakes and how he lets the bike run to the apex.
  • Wheelspin: watch what the rear wheel does, but don't conclude that more spin is faster in itself.
  • Exit: watch how early the rider can pick the bike up and put the power down.
  • Context: tyres, electronics, aerodynamics, set-up and track conditions change what is possible. Compare similar corners and bike generations whenever you can.
Conclusion: Stoner and Márquez share adaptability and exceptional speed in certain types of corner, but they are not the same rider. The fairest comparison combines statistics, their own statements and footage in context — rather than declaring a winner from a single frame.
